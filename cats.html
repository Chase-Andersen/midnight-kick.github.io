Cats

Cat Nutrition Through the Life Stages

Cats were first domesticated around 7500 BCE in the western Asia region and are currently the second most popular domestic pet in the United States. While there are over 60 unique documented cat breeds, care and diet can differ between breeds. However, some diets and practices are generally recommended for the well-being of your fluffy family member regardless of breed.

Zero to Four Weeks

It is important the queen (a term commonly used for a female cat that is either pregnant or nursing) directly nurse her young if possible. Monitor your kitten’s growth closely to make sure its growth rate is progressing steadily. If any kitten is not growing at a sufficient rate, a caretaker might need to feed the kitten directly either with a bottle or a feeding tube.

Some reasons why kittens might not gain weight appropriately include:

many siblings competing for mom's milk. Gastrointestinal disease. Environmental stress (extreme heat, cold, or unsanitary conditions)

Four Weeks to One Year

Kittens can start being introduced to soft wet kitten food around three to four weeks after birth. According to the ASPCA, kittens at this age should eat ½ to 1 cup of dry kitten food or 6–9 ounces of wet kitten food per day.

If your kitten has trouble eating dry food, you can soften it with a small amount of water. Kitten-specific food provides the extra nutrients needed for growth, energy, and wellness. Depending on the breed, dietary requirements may vary, so always consult your veterinarian.

One Year to Seven Years

At this stage, cats no longer need the extra nutrients of kittens. Their activity level and metabolism begin to slow down.

Avoid leaving food out all day—this can lead to obesity. Instead, serve meals a couple times a day to promote healthy eating habits and reduce weight-related issues.

Seven Years and Older

As cats age, their bodies change significantly. Senior cats should eat less fat and calories, but higher-quality protein. Look for food that specifies a protein source (e.g., “salmon”) rather than generic terms (e.g., “fish”), as the latter often indicates lower-quality byproducts.
